Six layers, in this order. Each one only reads the layers before it. churn_labels needs no cleaning (no duplicates, no invalid values), so it is used as delivered.

Grain: one row per subscription period

The period is the unit the business sells and prices, so discount, plan length, auto-renew and upgrade/downgrade live naturally on it. Episodes are what churn is about, so every period carries episode_id, episode_start_date, episode_end_date and episode_outcome.

Key fact behind the dates: expires_at is the membership expiry after the event, not the plan’s own end. An early renewal stacks its days on top of the old expiry, so each period also gets non-overlapping covered_start / covered_end (0 overlaps). A new episode starts when the gap after expiry is more than 30 days.

Known limits

  • The same-day order (cancel before purchase) is a rule, not a fact: the files have no timestamp or event ID. It is documented, deterministic and has one data-driven exception (rule 7)
  • T16/T17 derived expiries use purchase date + plan days. For an early renewal the true expiry would be later; this affects 1,691 rows
  • March-2017 churn is censored by construction (the 30-day window needs April data). Billing's labels use April data we don't have; Part 1c deals with that
  • Cancel-row amounts (rule 16) are interpreted, not documented. If billing confirms they are refunds, revenue needs a negative adjustment

Bottom line: Churn is about how people pay and how new they are, not who they are. Auto-renew and the first renewal matter most. Age, city and channel look important, but most of their gap disappears once we compare people with the same auto-renew setting and plan.

Two factors stay strong after a fair comparison

Each pair of bars shows how many times higher churn is in a factor's worst group than in its best group. Grey: as it looks. Blue: after comparing people with the same auto-renew setting and plan length. Auto-renew (4.5×), payment method (4.4×) and tenure (3.5×) keep a big gap. Age drops from 3.9× to 1.3×: it only looked like a driver.

Groups with fewer than 10,000 renewals are left out of the gap. Auto-renew is compared within the same plan length; plan length within the same auto-renew setting.

Age is a mirage

Under-25s churn 2–3× more, but only 33–54% of them use auto-renew (vs 84–90% of users 25 and older). With the same payment mix, every age band churns 4.2–5.6%.

Channel is mostly mix too

Channel 4 churns 8× more than channel 7 as it looks. Channel 7 users are 99% auto-renew, channel 4 only 38%. With the same mix the gap shrinks to about 1.5×.

The first renewal is the riskiest moment

16.5% leave at their first renewal, then churn settles at about 4%. It is not just payment mix: even with auto-renew, first-timers churn 8.7% vs about 2% later. Tenure is a real driver.

Tenure = months since the user's first paid subscription. Users who paid before 2015 show a shorter tenure than they really have.

Long plans lose more per renewal, far less per month

All long plans are paid manually, so we compare manual payers only. A 12-month plan loses 30.7% at renewal, but that renewal comes once a year: 2.7% a month, vs 18.5% a month on manual 30-day plans. Plan length only looks bad when you count renewals, not months.

Per month = 1 − (1 − churn per renewal)30 / plan days. People who choose a yearly plan may be more committed to begin with, so this is not proof that annual plans keep people (see Part 5a test).

One payment-provider incident, not a customer trend

Payment method 39 lost 47% of renewals in Q1 2016 and 84% in Q2, then fell back to 2%, like every other auto-renew method. 97% of those users came back by July 2016 (Part 2). We left these 39,323 renewals out of the factor ranking above.

3a · What We Would Act On

Three moves aimed at the drivers that survive a fair comparison, and what we would leave alone

Bottom line: Fix the payment and the first month, not the audience. Together these three moves could keep up to ~13,000 more subscribers a year if each lands as estimated (about 4% of today's 303,000 paying base).

1 · Move manual payers to auto-renew

Manual payers churn 20.1% per renewal vs 2.3% on auto-renew, still 4.5× after a fair comparison. They are 13% of renewals but 57% of all churn.

Expected impact
Every 10% of manual payers moved keeps about 3,000 subscribers a year, if they churn only half as much as today (not as little as current auto-renew users)
Confidence
Medium Committed users may choose auto-renew in the first place, so part of the gap is who they are. Test it: auto-renew on by default at checkout
Would change our mind
A test where switching auto-renew on does not lower churn, or raises refunds and complaints

2 · Win the first renewal

16.5% leave at their first renewal vs ~4% afterwards, and 8.7% even with auto-renew. Onboarding, a reminder of progress before the first charge, and a learning goal in week one.

Expected impact
Cutting first-renewal churn by a quarter keeps about 400 a month, ~4,800 a year.
Confidence
Medium The gap is large and survives the fair comparison; how much of it onboarding can move is unknown
Would change our mind
First-month leavers turn out to be discount or trial hunters who never meant to stay

3 · Watch failed payments per provider, every day

One provider (method 39) lost 16,468 users in a single month in 2016. Among auto-renew users, method 40 still churns 4.3% vs 1.9% on method 41.

Expected impact
Bringing method 40 to method 41's level keeps about 460 a month, ~5,500 a year; an early alert would have caught the 2016 incident in days, not months
Confidence
Medium-high for the incident, medium for method 40 (its users may differ in other ways)
Would change our mind
Billing logs showing these were deliberate cancellations, not failed charges

Analysis SQL (clean layer + a hypothetical experiment_assignments(user_id, variant, assigned_at) table; tested on a fake assignment):

with a as (
  select e.user_id, e.variant, e.assigned_at::date as d0, max(s.is_auto_renew) as is_auto_renew
  from experiment_assignments e
  left join fct_subscription s
    on s.user_id = e.user_id and e.assigned_at::date between s.start_date and s.end_date
  group by all
), u as (
  select a.user_id, a.variant, a.is_auto_renew,
    max((s.plan_days >= 360 and s.start_date between a.d0 and a.d0 + 37)::int)            as took_annual,
    max((s.is_free = 0 and s.start_date between a.d0 and a.d0 + 37)::int)                 as renewed,
    coalesce(sum(s.amount_paid) filter (where s.start_date between a.d0 and a.d0 + 364), 0) as cash_12m,
    coalesce(sum(s.amount_paid * greatest(0, least(s.end_date, a.d0 + 90) - greatest(s.start_date, a.d0))
                 / nullif(s.end_date - s.start_date, 0)), 0)                               as earned_90d
  from a left join fct_subscription s on s.user_id = a.user_id and s.start_date >= a.d0
  group by all
), g as (
  select is_auto_renew, variant, count(*) n, avg(took_annual) take_up, avg(renewed) renewal,
         avg(cash_12m) cash, var_samp(cash_12m) v, avg(earned_90d) earned_90d
  from u group by all
)
select t.is_auto_renew, t.n n_treat, c.n n_ctrl, t.take_up, c.renewal ctrl_renewal, t.renewal treat_renewal,
       t.cash - c.cash as cash_diff,
       (t.cash - c.cash) / sqrt(t.v / t.n + c.v / c.n) as z_score,   -- |z| > 1.96 = significant
       t.earned_90d - c.earned_90d as earned_90d_diff
from g t join g c on c.is_auto_renew = t.is_auto_renew and t.variant = 'treatment' and c.variant = 'control';
Limits:
  • Baselines come from 2016; prices and the payer mix may have moved since
  • We can't see app usage, so we can't tell whether annual buyers stay because they learn more or because they prepaid
  • The 69.0% annual renewal comes from people who chose annual on their own; discount takers may renew less

4 · Executive summary

For the CEO and the Head of Growth. The four leadership questions, answered as far as the data allows. Every number comes from Part 1 (data), Part 2 (metrics) and Part 3 (churn deep dive).

Bottom line

The subscriber base is healthy but growth is slowing Paying subscribers grew +19% in the last year (from +52% the year before), and monthly churn fell to about 3.6%. 1 · How healthy is the base? Answered

Healthy, growing, but slowing

  • +19% paying subscribers in the last year, down from +52%
  • Monthly churn mostly 3–5%; 3.6% in 2017
  • Renewing users are 94–96% of the base; new payers fell to 2.9% a month
  • Revenue per paying user down about 10% since 2015

2 · What drives churn? Answered

How people pay, and how new they are

  • Auto-renew: off 20.1% vs on 2.3% per renewal (4.5× after a fair comparison)
  • First renewal: 16.5% vs ~4% later (3.5× after a fair comparison)
  • Payment provider: one billing incident (Mar 2016, 16,468 users), plus a real gap: method 40 4.3% vs method 41 1.9%
  • Age, city and channel only look important: their gap shrinks to 1.3–2.1× once auto-renew and plan length are equal

3 · Did free trials and prices work? Partly

We see what changed, not whether it paid off

  • Discounts stopped by Oct 2015: from 9.7% of paid purchases to about 0.1%
  • Promotion moved to free trials: 42,743 free starts in Jun 2015, 58,821 in Oct–Nov 2016
  • Not measured: our deep dive chose churn drivers (option a), so the trial and price test (option b) is not done

4 · Push annual plans harder? Partly

Promising, but test before pushing

  • Per month of cover, manual annual plans lose 2.7% vs 18.5% on manual 30-day plans
  • But committed users pick annual plans, so this is not proof: that is the Part 5a test
  • Annual plans are 10.7% of revenue; do not judge them on billing's labels (97.7% of 90+ day renewals wrongly "churned")
